EyeCtrl Design System

Overlay unification — v2.2 — three themes, nine gates
Theme
Flip the switch above. Every component on this page is written once against CSS variables; the switch swaps the token values and nothing else — so the page is a working demonstration of the token layer it documents. Midnight is the shipping palette (spec §4.1); Ember is the warm low-blue-light alternative (§10) and Contrast is a situational high-contrast theme — both additive and not yet implemented. All three pass all nine gates: python tools/palette_validate.py. Visual sections follow the switch; measured tables always show both themes, so nothing is hidden behind a toggle.

In Contrast the fill steps are ΔE 3.7 and 5.6 — effectively invisible — and BorderSubtle at 21:1 against a 3:1 floor carries the layering single-handed. Gate 1 passes there for a different reason than in the other two themes: structure by outline, not by fill. That is the authentic high-contrast idiom.

Elevation raises opacity — SurfaceLight is fully opaque, SurfaceDark the most translucent. v1.0 had this inverted at 72% / 90%, making modals the least separated surface. Surface opacity also went 90% → 98%, cutting composited contrast drift from −27% to −5%; real translucency now lives only in ModalBackdrop and the full-screen dims.

Both secondary text tokens moved up from v1.0, where TextSecondary was large-text-only and TextDim failed every threshold at 2.76:1 — yet both are used on small text. The accent is reserved — it means exactly one thing, gaze dwell in progress, and nothing meaning-bearing sits within ΔE2000 25 of it. v1.0 spent two near-identical light blues on "dwelling" (#7DCFFF) and "focused" (#7AA2F7), ΔE 14.8 apart and collapsing to 8.6 under tritanopia. Selection and active state are now carried by fill, so BorderFocus is simply Accent. Separate the channel, not the hue.
Midnight and Ember share the semantic triad, by design. Success, warning and danger encode outcome, not brand, so they need not vary — theme identity lives in surfaces, text and the accent. One fewer triad to re-validate, and §9.4's measurements transfer unchanged.

Contrast cannot share it, and that is a finding rather than an inconsistency: #FF0000 is only 5.25:1 on black, so a high-contrast theme has to lighten its red to #FF7777 (8.16:1) — which, at C* 57.6, only just clears gate 9's chroma floor. Push further for contrast and it stops reading as red. Its triad is marked ·HC-specific when that theme is active.
Semantic colour is never the sole carrier of meaning. The triad is staged by lightness (L* 83.6 / 72.0 / 61.9) so it degrades gracefully, but warning and danger still collapse under tritanopia (ΔE 1.6) — unsolvable with one amber and one red on the blue-yellow axis. Every use must be paired with a distinct glyph (✓ / ⚠ / ✕). For scale: v1.0's success and warning sat at ΔE 1.1 under deuteranopia — the same colour for roughly 6% of males.
High contrast and colour-blind robustness pull against each other. In Contrast, #00FF00 and #FFFF00 collapse to ΔE 2.7 under protanopia — against Midnight's 13.3 for the same pair. Maximum saturation pushes colours toward the confusion-line endpoints, so the very property that makes the theme high-contrast makes its hues less separable for CVD. Gate 5 passes there only on its lightness criterion (ΔL* 9.40, a 1.28:1 luminance ratio). The glyph rule is more load-bearing in Contrast, not less — for a protanope, ✓ versus ⚠ is very nearly the only thing separating success from warning.
Contrast is situational, not a daily driver. Pure white at 21:1 for hours is exactly what Midnight's TextPrimary deliberately avoids (§4.1.2 rejects #FFFFFF for that reason). It is for glare, a bright room, or tired eyes. Its pure-black surfaces are also the friendliest of the three to the eye tracker, so it costs nothing on that axis. Note too that its surfaces are opaque — translucency and a contrast guarantee are incompatible — which is why "elevation raises opacity" reads a trivial 255→255 and the worst-composited case equals the surface itself.

4. StrokeHalo — transparent-overlay contrast

CursorCircleWindow, ActivationButtonWindow, ManualSelectionGazeIndicatorWindow and ScrollOverlayWindow draw straight onto desktop content with no surface beneath them. No single colour can hold contrast there — today's Lime ring falls to 1.37:1 over a white document, and any replacement fares the same. This is a contrast-guarantee problem, not a palette problem, and v1.0 had it out of scope while these are the app's primary dwell feedback.

Every accent stroke on a transparent overlay is drawn twice: a StrokeHalo outline at StrokeThickness + 2, then the Accent stroke over it. The visible feature becomes the internal edge, which is fixed regardless of backdrop. Left ring in each cell is single-stroke, middle is today's Lime, right is haloed.

The accent swap does not regress

Contrast against each theme's own opaque core, as each vision type perceives it (Viénot 1999). v1.0's #7DCFFF was worse than Lime on all four rows — the claim that the two had "nearly identical" contrast was wrong, and that swap would have degraded the single signal the interaction model depends on.

Gate 9 exists because gates 1–8 were gameable. A hue sweep over the Ember design space produced arrangements that passed all eight original gates with a near-white #F0D6D1 as warning and a pastel pink as danger — numerically fine, semantically meaningless. Nothing required a semantic colour to be saturated enough to signify. Gate 9 is a Lab chroma floor of C* ≥ 55 on the triad. It is scoped to the triad only: Accent is correctly low-chroma (C* ≈ 25 in both themes), being a luminance signal rather than a categorical one.
A corrected claim, recorded. An earlier draft said Ember's AccentDanger was stuck at 4.12:1 (AA-large) and that this was structural. That was wrong. Sweeping accent hue with warm surfaces and text held fixed found arrangements well above AA: the cause was a choice, not a constraint — a gold accent spent the warm hue axis, leaving the semantics competing with it for ΔE 25. Ember now uses a cool accent on warm surfaces, and its danger is 5.74:1, identical to Midnight. The accent covers a few percent of lit pixels, so keeping it cool costs almost nothing in short-wavelength exposure — which is dominated by surfaces, and those stay warm.

8. Usage patterns

Tokens live in EyeCtrl.Controls, not EyeCtrl.App. The reference direction is App → Controls → Core, so a dictionary under src/EyeCtrl.App/ would be invisible to DwellArcButton and the other shared dwell controls — v1.0's Phase 1 could not have compiled. Theme/Tokens.cs is the single source of truth; the XAML dictionary derives from it via x:Static, so drift between the two views is structurally impossible. Plain C# statics rather than Application.Current.Resources, because EyeCtrl.Controls.Tests runs without a WPF Application.

In XAML

<!-- App.xaml, once -->
<ResourceDictionary Source="pack://application:,,,/
  EyeCtrl.Controls;component/Theme/OverlayStyles.xaml"/>

<!-- Dictionary entry — one literal, in Tokens.cs -->
<SolidColorBrush x:Key="BrushSurfaceDark"
    Color="{x:Static theme:Tokens.SurfaceDark}"/>

<!-- Before -->
<Border Background="#F0181818" CornerRadius="8"/>

<!-- After -->
<Border Background="{StaticResource BrushSurfaceDark}"
    CornerRadius="{StaticResource CornerRadiusStandard}"
    BorderBrush="{StaticResource BrushBorderSubtle}"
    BorderThickness="{StaticResource BorderThicknessStandard}"/>

In C# code-behind

// Before — magic literal, repeated across 6 files
new SolidColorBrush(
    Color.FromArgb(200, 60, 60, 80));

// After — one source of truth
Tokens.BrushSurfaceMedium;

// Halo pass on a transparent overlay.
// Declare the halo BEFORE the accent stroke.
Halo.Stroke = Tokens.BrushStrokeHalo;
Halo.StrokeThickness = t + 2;
Ring.Stroke = Tokens.BrushAccent;
Ring.StrokeThickness = t;

Brushes are Freeze()d statics, so the halo adds one shape per overlay and no per-frame allocation on the gaze path. Watch z-order: inverted, the halo renders as a dark ring on every backdrop — obvious to the eye, invisible to every headless test.

A second theme changes the resource contract. The migration plan specifies {StaticResource}, which resolves once when a window loads. That is fine for a theme chosen at startup and for overlays created per use, but a live switch affecting an already-open Settings or Keyboard window needs {DynamicResource} or window recreation. Decide restart-vs-live before Phase 4 — converting those references afterwards touches all eleven files in that phase. Tokens.cs should also be an ITheme implementation from Phase 0, with the statics as Midnight's, rather than being refactored later.
Four of these colours are persisted settings, not XAML. CursorSettings.CircleColor, CalibrationSettings.TargetColor, AutoSelectionSettings.HighlightColor and DragSettings.TrailColor live in EyeCtrl.Core and are written to %APPDATA%\EyeCtrl\settings.json. The first two are read back over the XAML value at runtime, so a XAML-only edit is a no-op for the cursor ring and calibration target. Per spec §7.1 there is deliberately no migration code: three of the four sections are absent from the existing file and inherit the new defaults automatically, and cursor.circleColor is set to #0088FF — worth changing once via the picker, since at 5.59:1 against StrokeHalo it drops the §4 edge guarantee below its 7:1 gate. Every colour-picker preset must clear 7:1 against StrokeHalo, and the halos differ per theme.
